Before We Begin · Lesson 01
Who Was
Benjamin Graham?
Benjamin Graham (1894–1976) is the father of value investing. He taught at Columbia Business School for decades. His most famous student was Warren Buffett — who called Graham the second most influential person in his life after his own father.
🎓
The Teacher
Taught Warren Buffett, Irving Kahn, and Walter Schloss. His class produced some of the greatest investors in history. He taught that investing is a discipline — not a talent.
📉
The Survivor
Graham personally lost almost everything in the 1929 stock market crash. He wrote this book so that ordinary people would never have to make the same mistakes. Every lesson is earned, not borrowed.
📖
The Book
First published in 1949. Revised in 1959, 1965, and 1972. Updated by Jason Zweig in 2003 with modern commentary after each chapter. Still the most recommended investing book in the world.
🧠
The Philosophy
Graham did not teach people how to get rich quickly. He taught people how to think correctly about money, business, and risk — so they would never become poor through their own mistakes.

Chapter 01 · Lesson 03
Investment
vs Speculation
The most important distinction in all of finance — and most people get it wrong
Graham's Definition — Memorise This
"An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative."
The Investor Thinks
What is this business worth?
  • Analyses the actual business behind the stock
  • Calculates what the company is worth
  • Only buys below that value with a cushion
  • Holds patiently — months or years
  • Sees price drops as potential opportunities
  • Focuses on safety of capital first
The Speculator Thinks
What will others pay tomorrow?
  • Bets on short-term price movements
  • Follows trends, tips and headlines
  • Buys because others are buying
  • Holds for days, hours or minutes
  • Sees price drops as disasters
  • Focuses on profit first — risk ignored
The Key Insight
The same stock can be bought as an investment or speculated upon — depending entirely on the buyer's thinking process. A person who analyses the business and pays below its value is an investor. A person who buys the same stock because it went up yesterday is a speculator.
Chapter 02 · Lesson 04
The Investor
& Inflation
Why keeping money in cash is the riskiest strategy of all
Most people think keeping cash in a savings account is the safe option. Graham shows this is an illusion. Inflation silently destroys the purchasing power of cash every year. The real question is not "can I lose money in cash?" — it is "how much value am I losing every year by holding cash?"
💸
The Cash Trap
At 3% annual inflation, $10,000 in cash becomes worth only $7,374 in real purchasing power after 10 years. You see $10,000 on paper — but it buys 26% less than it used to.
📈
Stocks vs Inflation
Businesses with pricing power can raise their prices when costs rise. Their earnings grow in nominal terms. Shareholders benefit — their ownership stake grows in real value.
📉
Bond Danger
A bond pays a fixed interest rate. If inflation rises above that rate, the bondholder loses in real terms. Graham warned against excessive bond exposure for long-term investors.
The best defence against inflation is a portfolio of productive assets — businesses that produce real goods or services whose prices rise with the general cost of living.
— Graham's Inflation Principle
What This Means Practically
Graham recommended that the defensive investor hold at least 25% in stocks at all times — never going entirely to cash or bonds even when afraid. The pain of a temporary market drop is always less than the permanent loss of purchasing power from inflation.
Chapters 03–05 · Lesson 05
The Defensive
Investor
For those who want good returns with minimum effort and maximum safety
Graham divides investors into two types. The defensive investor wants to avoid serious mistakes, minimise effort, and achieve satisfactory results. This is actually the correct approach for the vast majority of people.
The Defensive Investor's Four Rules
01
Adequate diversification — own between 10 and 30 stocks
No single stock should be large enough to hurt you seriously if it collapses. A broad index fund like an S&P 500 ETF is the ultimate implementation of this rule — owning 500 stocks automatically.
Modern equivalent: a low-cost S&P 500 index fund covers this rule completely.
02
Only large, prominent, conservatively financed companies
Defensive investors should avoid small, speculative, or heavily indebted companies. Stick to businesses that have survived multiple economic cycles and have strong balance sheets.
Think: companies with 20+ year operating histories, low debt, consistent profits.
03
Long, uninterrupted record of dividend payments
Graham considered a consistent dividend record as evidence that the business generates real cash and that management is honest with shareholders. A company that has paid dividends for 20+ years has survived recessions, wars, and crises.
04
Never pay more than 25× recent average earnings
Even the greatest business becomes a bad investment if you pay too much. Graham's PE limit prevents the defensive investor from making speculative purchases dressed up as quality investments.
Paying PE of 60× for a "great company" is speculation — not investment.
Chapters 06–07 · Lesson 06
The Enterprising
Investor
More work, more potential reward — but also far more discipline required
The enterprising investor dedicates significant time to research in exchange for above-average returns. Graham warns — most people who think they are enterprising investors are actually just more aggressive speculators who do not know it yet.
Graham's Honest Warning
The enterprising investor must genuinely outperform the passive index fund investor — otherwise, all the extra effort produces worse results than doing nothing. Most professional fund managers fail this test.
Active Strategies Graham Approves
✓ What the Enterprising Investor Does
  • Searches for genuinely undervalued individual stocks
  • Reads financial statements — balance sheet, income statement, cash flow
  • Buys stocks trading well below calculated intrinsic value
  • Identifies turnaround situations with strong underlying businesses
  • Holds cash when no attractive opportunity exists
  • Runs a focused portfolio of 8–15 deeply researched stocks
Strategies Graham Permanently Forbids
✗ What No Investor Should Do
  • Day trading — buying and selling within hours or days
  • Buying hot new IPOs at launch price
  • Investing based on tips from friends, media, or analysts
  • Buying "growth stocks" at any price regardless of value
  • Using borrowed money (margin) to amplify bets
  • Trying to time the market's direction
Chapter 08 · The Most Important Lesson · Lesson 07
Mr Market —
The Parable
One story that will protect your wealth for the rest of your life
Graham asks you to imagine that you own a share in a private business. Your business partner — Mr Market — shows up at your door every single day. Every day he names a price at which he will buy your share from you, or sell you his share. He is always happy to do either.
The problem is that Mr Market is emotionally unstable. Some days he is wildly optimistic and offers an absurdly high price. Other days he is deeply pessimistic and offers a very low price. His price has nothing to do with the actual value of the business — only with his mood that day.
When Mr Market is Depressed
He names a very low price. He is scared. He sees only danger. If you know the business is healthy and worth more — this is your buying opportunity. His panic is your profit.
When Mr Market is Euphoric
He names a very high price. He is excited. He sees only opportunity. If you know the business is worth less than he is offering — sell to him gladly. His greed is your exit.
You are under no obligation to trade with Mr Market just because he shows up. You can ignore him every day until the day he is irrational enough in your favour. That is your entire job as an intelligent investor.
— Benjamin Graham, Chapter 8
The Permanent Lesson
The stock price is Mr Market's daily opinion of what your shares are worth. His opinion changes every minute based on news, emotion, and the mood of crowds. The intrinsic value of the business changes slowly — over months and years based on actual earnings and cash flows. Never confuse the two.
Market
Chapters 09–11 · Lesson 08
Portfolio Policy
& Dollar Cost Averaging
How to construct and manage your holdings over time
Graham establishes clear rules for how to build and maintain a portfolio. He also delivers a verdict on professional fund managers that shocked the industry: most of them underperform a simple index fund after fees.
Graham's Three Portfolio Principles
1
Adjust stock and bond allocation based on valuations
Graham recommended a 50/50 split as a baseline. Shift toward more stocks (up to 75%) when markets are cheap relative to earnings. Shift toward more bonds (up to 75%) when markets are expensive. This is the opposite of what most investors do — they buy more when markets are high and sell when low.
When P/E ratios are high market-wide → hold more cash. When cheap → increase stocks.
2
Dollar-cost averaging — invest a fixed amount regularly
Invest a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals — monthly, quarterly — regardless of whether the market is up or down. This automatically causes you to buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when high. Over time, your average cost is better than anyone who tries to time the market.
Monthly $500 invested for 10 years regardless of market conditions. The method works automatically.
3
Index funds beat most active managers over the long term
Graham's data showed that after fees, most actively managed funds underperform the simple market index over 10+ years. The reason is fees, trading costs, and the impossibility of consistently being right. A low-cost index fund guarantees you capture the full market return — which beats the majority of professionals.
Most fund managers underperform their benchmark after 10 years. The data is overwhelming.

Security Analysis
& Stock Selection
How to read a business — not just its stock price
Graham teaches the fundamentals of evaluating a company's financial health. The key insight: most investors look at a stock's price chart and recent price movement. The intelligent investor reads the financial statements and evaluates the underlying business.

Graham's Combined Multiplier Rule
Graham's shortcut: multiply the PE ratio by the Price-to-Book ratio. The result should not exceed 22.5. This ensures you never pay an excessive premium on both earnings and assets simultaneously — a double safety check.
Chapters 14–15 · Lesson 10
Earnings Per Share
& Earnings Quality
Not all profits are equal — learning to read numbers honestly
Graham devotes significant attention to how company earnings can be manipulated or misleading. Reported profit is an accounting number — it can differ significantly from the actual cash the business generates. The intelligent investor checks both.
✓
High-Quality Earnings
Free Cash Flow closely matches reported Net Income. The company converts most of its profit into actual cash. Non-GAAP adjustments are small, consistent, and clearly explained. Accounting is conservative rather than aggressive.
✗
Low-Quality Earnings
Free Cash Flow is far below Net Income. The company is booking profit on paper that is not arriving in cash. Non-GAAP adjustments are large and growing. Management adds back recurring costs as "one-time" items every single year.
Graham's Five Earnings Checks
1
Compare FCF to Net Income — should be within 10–15% of each other
2
Check how revenue is recognised — when earned or when contracted?
3
Count non-GAAP "one-time" charges — if recurring every year, they are not one-time
4
Check share count trend — is management diluting shareholders or buying back shares?
5
Read the footnotes — that is where the problems are always hidden
Core Concept · Lesson 11
Intrinsic Value —
What Is a Business Worth?
The concept that separates all investors from all speculators
Intrinsic value is the actual worth of a business — calculated from its earnings, assets, growth prospects, and competitive position — independent of what its stock price is today. Graham makes a sharp distinction: the price is what you pay, the value is what you get.
🏷️
Price
What Mr Market offers you today. Changes every minute. Driven by emotion, news, and supply and demand. Completely irrelevant to the actual worth of the business. The speculator focuses here.
⚖️
Value
What the business is actually worth based on its earnings power and assets. Changes slowly — over months and years based on fundamental business performance. The intelligent investor focuses here.
How Graham Calculated Intrinsic Value
1
Earnings Power — what does the business earn normalised over time?
Use average earnings over 5–10 years rather than a single year. One exceptional year or one terrible year should not drive the valuation. Stability and consistency matter.
2
Apply a reasonable multiplier — Graham used 8.5 to 15× earnings
The multiplier reflects the quality of the earnings — how consistent, how likely to grow, how defensible. Better businesses deserve higher multipliers. But Graham sets a ceiling: never pay more than 25× earnings under any circumstances.
3
Check against asset value — what are the net assets worth?
Book value — the net assets of the business — provides a second check. Graham preferred to buy below book value when possible, as this provided extra protection if the business did poorly.
Value
Chapter 20 · The Most Important Chapter · Lesson 12
"Margin of Safety"
as the Central Concept
The three words that separate investors from gamblers — forever
The Definition — The Entire Book in One Sentence
Margin of Safety = the difference between the price you pay and the intrinsic value of what you buy. Always buy below intrinsic value. The gap between what you pay and what it is worth is your protection against being wrong.
Graham uses the analogy of a civil engineer. When designing a bridge to carry 10,000 tonnes, he builds it to safely carry 30,000 tonnes. The extra 20,000 tonnes is not wasted — it is the margin of safety that protects against calculation errors, unexpected stress, and unforeseen problems. Intelligent investing works exactly the same way.
Why You Need It
No analysis is perfectly accurate. Businesses face unexpected challenges. Even the best investor is wrong sometimes. The margin of safety absorbs your errors so a mistake becomes a small loss rather than a disaster.
How Big Should It Be?
Graham required a minimum 33% margin of safety. Pay no more than 67 cents for every dollar of intrinsic value. The riskier or more uncertain the business, the larger the margin required.
What Destroys It
Paying a high price for "a great company." Even the finest business provides no margin of safety if you pay 50× earnings for it. Overpaying turns investment into speculation.
The function of the margin of safety is, in essence, that of rendering unnecessary an accurate estimate of the future. If the margin is large enough, it is sufficient to assume that future earnings will not fall far below those of the past in order for an investor to feel sufficiently protected against the vicissitudes of time.
— Benjamin Graham, Chapter 20
Psychology of Investing · Lesson 13
The Investor's
Greatest Enemy
Graham's most honest chapter — the problem is us, not the market
Graham concludes his analysis of every strategy, every formula, and every technique with a humbling observation: the biggest obstacle to investment success is not finding the right stocks. It is managing your own emotions. The investor's chief problem is likely to be himself.
The Five Emotional Errors Graham Identified
1
Speculation disguised as investment
Buying a stock because the price went up recently and feeling clever about it. Selling a stock because the price went down and calling it "risk management." Both are emotion — neither is analysis.
2
Trading too much — action for its own sake
Every trade has a cost. Every unnecessary trade transfers your money to brokers and market makers. Graham found that the most active traders consistently underperformed the least active ones. Doing nothing is often the correct action.
3
Following the crowd at the worst times
Markets reach their highest prices when optimism is maximum — exactly when the future expected returns are lowest. Markets reach their lowest when fear is maximum — when future returns are highest. The crowd consistently does the opposite of what is rational.
4
Abandoning good strategy during difficulty
Every sound strategy goes through periods of underperformance. The investor who abandons their strategy at the first sign of difficulty will never succeed — because they will always sell at the bottom and buy at the top of each cycle.
5
Confusing luck with skill
In a bull market, almost any stock purchase profits. This tricks investors into believing they have superior skill. When the market turns, they discover they were just positioned correctly for that cycle — not analytically superior. Humility is protective.

Synthesis · Lesson 14
Graham's Five
Permanent Rules
Everything in 641 pages distilled to what you must never forget
Ⅰ
A stock is ownership of a real business — always think like an owner
The moment you forget that a stock certificate represents a fractional ownership of a real company with real employees, real products, and real earnings — you become a speculator. Ask always: "Would I buy the entire business at this price?"
Ⅱ
The market is your servant — never your guide
Mr Market's daily price quotations are an offer — not a command. You are never obligated to sell because the price fell or buy because it rose. Use the market's volatility to your advantage rather than being ruled by it.
Ⅲ
Always demand a margin of safety — never overpay
The three words that summarise the entire book. No matter how great the business, pay below its intrinsic value. The gap between price paid and value received is your cushion against errors, bad luck, and unforeseen events.
Graham: Pay no more than 67 cents for every dollar of intrinsic value.
Ⅳ
Be fearful when others are greedy — greedy when others are fearful
The best buying opportunities arise exactly when fear is highest and the crowd is selling. The most dangerous times to buy are when optimism is universal and prices reflect perfection. This is counter-intuitive — and that is precisely why so few people do it.
Ⅴ
Patience and discipline are the only edges available to ordinary investors
You cannot out-analyse Wall Street. You cannot trade faster than computers. But you can be more patient. You can hold when they panic. You can wait when they chase. Time is the ordinary investor's only sustainable competitive advantage.
The stock market transfers wealth from the impatient to the patient — every single time.
